My latest projects

Updated as I ship new ones

A jamu-inspired snack brand with three products: jamu layered cake, jamu crackers and jamu kohakuto. My team built it in Batch 3 of Kelas Pagi Pangan, from October to December 2026.

About the class: Kelas Pagi Pangan is a food class started by photographer Anton Ismael, who has run the free photography school Kelas Pagi since 2006. It treats food as a way to understand culture. Participants take a traditional Indonesian dish apart and rebuild it into something that feels relevant today and has real business potential.

The brand came with it: identity, a packaging sleeve, a booth concept, and a story card about the spices for every purchase.

My part: the concept, the brand guideline and the pitch, plus the design work: the hand-drawn logo and jamu-seller icon, the cake sleeve, and the labels for the kohakuto and crackers. We worked as a team; these were the parts I drove.

Why jamu

Jamu is fading UNESCO listed Indonesia's jamu culture as heritage in 2023, yet only 35% of Gen Z drink it, against 68% of Gen X.
The problem is the form Young people expect it to be bitter, dislike the smell, and find it old-fashioned.
So we changed the form Of the 39 people we surveyed, 76.9% look for food they have never tried and 59% choose by looks. Familiar snacks answer both.

The client's problem

During COVID-19, Toyota Tsusho ran its own shuttle fleet with dedicated drivers to carry staff to and from work. What they lacked was a way to plan it: who gets picked up when, in what order, so that everyone reaches the office on time and gets home again afterwards.

What we built

Home addresses & shift times

Where each employee lives and when they need to be at work.

Routes & pickup times

Staff are grouped by location and given a pickup time that gets the whole van to the office before shift start.

Drivers & fleet

The client's own drivers run the route each morning, and the return leg each evening.

Dashboard for the company

Fleet utilization · Cost allocation per department · Driver working hours, used to calculate salary

Event formats

Each tour is a walking route with introductions, then dinner hopping between restaurants, priced pay-as-you-wish. We treat it like a product: test a format, see who shows up, keep what sells out.

What each side gets

For the people at the table

The food is only half of it. Every batch is built so strangers actually talk: introductions, games between stops, and a group small enough that nobody gets left out. People leave with new contacts, not just a full stomach.

For the brand

Real people trying the product in person: content shot with actual users, and honest reactions the brand can learn from. We wrap it in games, merchandise or a themed route, so the product gets experienced rather than explained. It works for something you can hold and for an app just as well.

How one batch feeds the next

Step 01 First I check if anyone wants it A new format opens with one "comment to join" post and 15–20 seats. Between 50 and 100 people sign up. So I know people want it before I build anything around it.
Step 02 Then I turn that crowd into something a brand can buy A brand doesn't want a post. It wants a moment with real people in it: a bank's food-hunt challenge, a camera trial on a photo walk, a route built for Independence Day. Same community, a different offer each time.
Step 03 Then the people who came do the marketing Everyone posts their own recap and tags the brand. One dinner becomes dozens of pieces of content the brand never had to make.

Those recaps are what fills the next batch. New people see them and sign up, and the brand sees its name in every one. Then it starts over.
